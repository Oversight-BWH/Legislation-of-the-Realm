Judicial Structure

The judiciary resolves civil and criminal disputes according to royal law, customary law, and ecclesiastical standards where applicable. It is designed to allow local disputes to be settled quickly while preserving higher review for matters of gravity or constitutional importance.

Level Authority Typical Matters
Local / Manorial Courts Lords, stewards, bailiffs, aldermen Small disputes, debt, trespass, local offenses
Town Courts Magistrates and guild officers Market disputes, merchant cases, public order
Provincial Courts Royal judges and provincial justices Major crime, inheritance, land dispute, felony
High Court Senior judicial officers Matters affecting state, nobility, treason, serious crimes
Crown Court Monarch or royal council High treason, succession disputes, extraordinary petitions

Courts of the Realm

Manorial Court

Resolved by the landholder or his steward. Handles disputes among tenants, inheritance questions, and local offenses.

Town Court

Deals with commerce, licenses, market regulation, property disputes, and offenses affecting local public order.

County Court

Hears broad civil and criminal matters subject to the sheriff or a royal justice. Deals with larger disputes or criminal accusations against commoners.

Provincial Court

Acts as major court of review and trial for serious crimes, important land claims, and higher repudiations of local decisions.

High Court of Justice

Composed of the most senior magistrates and royal appointees. Hears constitutional questions, treason, and cases affecting the Crown's authority.

Ecclesiastical Court

Deals with marriage, heresy, church property, clerical discipline, and matters in which religious law is the controlling authority.

Evidence & Proof

Judges require reliable proof before passing sentence. The standards of proof vary by offense and method of adjudication.

Witness Testimony

Witnesses are questioned under oath. False testimony is itself a crime and may lead to perjury charges.

Physical Evidence

Weapons, blood traces, marks of force, paperwork, stolen goods, and weapons connect the accused to the offense.

Confession

Confessions are persuasive but must be voluntary. Coerced or tortured confessions are not lawful evidence unless admitted under special authority.

Oath and Surety

Some disputes are decided by sworn testimony and the support of reputable guarantors. This is especially common in local civil matters.

Trial by Combat

Used in certain high-status or unresolved disputes where the law allows combat as a decisive proof. The victor is deemed the lawful party unless the outcome is overturned for fraud or breach of oath.

Modes of Trial

Trial by Witness

Most common in civil litigation and lower criminal cases. The judge weighs testimony and evidence.

Trial by Jury

Less common in feudal legal systems, but used in larger urban courts and selected major crimes when the law allows.

Trial by Combat

Used for disputes of honor, land contests, or where neither party can prove a claim with ordinary evidence. Lawful combat must be supervised and sanctioned.

Trial by Ordeal

Ancient method in which divine judgment is invoked through fire, water, or other tests. Often considered unreliable but may still be used in certain regions and traditions.

Trial by Inquisition / Ecclesiastical Examination

Used for charges of heresy, blasphemy, and doctrinal offense, particular where church authority is recognized as primary.

Appeals & Revision

Judgments may be challenged if the law was misapplied, evidence was wrongfully excluded, or the court acted beyond jurisdiction.

Appeal to Superior Court

Common in criminal and land disputes. The higher court reviews the record and may reverse, modify, or remand for retrial.

Pardon & Clemency

The Crown may commute sentences or pardon offenses in matters of mercy or public necessity. Pardon does not necessarily erase the underlying fact of guilt, but it may eliminate punishment.

Retrial

Allowed if new evidence arises or if the prior verdict was procured by fraud, bribery, or overt legal error.

Judges & Officers

Justice of the Peace

Responsible for hearing local criminal and civil matters, issuing warrants, and maintaining public order in the district.

Royal Judge

Appointed by the Crown to hear more serious appeals and disputes involving state interests or substantial property claims.

Provost Marshal

Military justice officer for armed forces and major operational conflict. Oversees court-martial and investigative matters.

Clerks of Court

Record legal motions, testimony, verdicts, and decrees. Vital in ensuring the continuity of judgment and appeal.

Bailiff / Marshal

Enforces court orders, protects the judge, summons parties, and carries out lawful detention and seizure.
